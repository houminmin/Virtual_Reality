# Chapter 3: Parting Ways

One weekend afternoon, Mike invited me to meet at a café in person. Virtual reality had long since become universal; most meetings and gatherings happened in VR, indistinguishable from meeting in person. I actually loved this stay-at-home way of meeting people. I could be disheveled and dressed in whatever, and with a brief description the VR would dress my avatar in Business, business casual, anything I liked—economical and convenient. That might be what I considered the greatest gift of VR technology. Occasionally, though, I still met people face to face—but only people I knew well, which is to say, people I knew wouldn't judge me by my appearance. Mike, my research partner, was one of those few.

The café we'd picked was one of the rare ones that still employed humans to make the coffee. Most people no longer went out for coffee at all—hardly anyone left the house, and the robots at home handled all food and drink. So this café was more like a tourist attraction. People came not for the coffee but to commemorate a bygone era, though when you thought about it, cafés with human baristas had still been everywhere just ten years ago.

I sat in a chair outside the café, drinking coffee and soaking in the sun. After being cooped up at home so long, it felt wonderful to be in the sun. I took a sip of coffee and lamented: "After watching so many hospice VR videos, I had to conclude that humans are pathetic. Driven by desires for fame and wealth, people lived a life so busy that they didn't have time to think about how they should have spent their lives when they were already at the end of them."

"You sound like such a cynic!" Mike laughed. "Without desire, without the pursuit of fame and fortune, human civilization would never have developed this far. We might still be in primitive tribes, eating raw meat."

"I'm not rejecting desire entirely. I'm saying we should recognize what excessive desire is—and learn to see and enjoy what we already have."

"Okay, okay, my philosopher..." Mike cut me off. "If everyone were the way you describe, we'd have no business. What I care about right now is how to grow the market. The more desires our users have, the better."

I had no comeback. Isn't the entire modern commercial economy built on humanity's bottomless desire? Merchants will even manufacture desire in consumers, all for profit. And now, thanks to the rock-bottom birth rate, the population kept shrinking, consumer markets kept contracting, and businesses pushed all the harder, ceaselessly cultivating each individual's appetite to consume. AI production costs were minimal—especially for a service like the Hospice Dream Factory, where serving a million users cost barely more than serving ten thousand, while revenue multiplied.

"Seriously, though—our product's user base is limited, and revenue has hit a ceiling. The money we raised is nearly gone. We need to raise more, or expand the market to grow revenue, or we can't keep operating." Mike turned serious.

"Meaning what, exactly?" I had a bad feeling.

"I want to expand the Dream Factory's user base," Mike said, looking at me calmly.

"No way!" I shot it down at once.

"Why?" Mike hadn't expected me to be so adamant.

"The moment the Dream Factory's user base extends beyond the dying, its harms will outweigh its benefits. Or rather—I can't see any benefit at all. Human desire is endless. In an illusion where desire is satisfied effortlessly, again and again, the pleasure will drown people. How is that different from a drug?" I was unyielding.

"But our balance sheet is what it is. You do know what your experiments on the human simulation model cost every month? Our monthly revenue barely covers operating costs—it can't pay for your experiments. Most of the VC money so far has gone into your research. If you want the human simulation model research to continue, we need a new source of funds." Mike didn't soften it. "To raise another round, we have to show a new revenue model and a new market. And if we don't raise, we still need a new market just to sustain both the Dream Factory's operations and your experiments."

I fell silent. The contradiction had been there from the beginning: my research had no commercial value but incurred enormous costs. Science enjoyed unprecedented esteem, yes—but only because technological progress made goods cheaper to produce and wealth faster to accumulate. Nobody cared about research for the pure sake of knowledge. Choosing commercial capital to keep my commercially worthless research alive was drinking poison to quench thirst—and I had naively fantasized that it would all work out.

I regretted agreeing to meet Mike in person. In a VR meeting I could have simply killed the video to end the conversation and register my anger. As it was, all I could do was stand up and walk away, leaving Mike glaring at my back, presumably fuming.

That night I lay in bed, tossing and turning. My human simulation experiments were at a critical point. If the funding stopped, everything would be lost. But to sustain the research on profits from an expanded Dream Factory—my conscience couldn't bear it, because I knew the harm the Dream Factory could do. Confucius said: do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself. That much conscience I still had to keep. Was there truly no other way? Was there truly no one else in the world who would be interested in this research?

The next day I called Mike. "Mike, hold off on expanding the user base for now. I'll find the money for my experiments myself."

Mike looked slightly stunned. He was silent for a long while, then said: "Actually—I've already decided to expand the user base. Whether or not you continue your experiments."

"Why?" Shock, anger, grief—everything surged up at once.

"What's so surprising? A company exists to make a profit. We built the most advanced technology; of course we should use it to create wealth. An enormous market sitting undeveloped—doesn't that strike you as a waste?"

"Shouldn't the most advanced technology be used for the good of society? Shouldn't creating wealth have a bottom line?" I demanded furiously.

"I want to expand the Dream Factory to help more people fulfill their wishes. How is that not good for society? How is that crossing a line?" Mike was angry now too. "Have you met people who cannot afford the Dream Factory? I did. Last month a woman wrote to me asking for a discount for her father who wished to see his late wife once more. I gave them a free trial."

I couldn't come up with a reply.

"If the user base is expanded, the economy of scale can cut the price to one tenth of the current price." Mike stared at me, "Your idealism cannot help them. My pragmatism can."

"But you know perfectly well what harm the Dream Factory could cause. Ask yourself honestly: if someone gets so addicted to the Dream Factory that something terrible happens—death, even—would you still expand the user base?" I pressed.

"You're blowing this out of proportion! Yes, the Dream Factory carries some risk. But if a thing is nine parts good and one part bad, do we throw away the nine for the one?"

"And what are the Dream Factory's good parts?"

"Fulfilling wishes, redeeming regrets, treating depression—plenty."

"Illusory fulfillment, illusory relief. You call that good?"

"Why are you so stubborn? What gives you the right to say the Dream Factory does no good? Do you have data? It's all just your imagination! If I don't do this business, someone else will. Better me than some profiteer." Mike was almost yelling.

Angry and disappointed, I hung up. Why keep arguing? Those who walk different paths cannot plan together.

A glass of water appeared in front of me—my robot, Ace. I took it, drank, looked out the window, and the tears came. I thought of everything Mike and I had built together over the past year and more, the shared goal we had once fought for, the trust and admiration I had for him, how I had counted him among my few true friends. Had I failed to see his nature clearly—or do human minds change so easily?

"People are too hard to understand! Robots are more reliable!" I handed the glass back to Ace, gradually calming down. Ace withdrew without a sound. It was a thoughtful companion; it knew my temperament—that I liked to think alone and hated being disturbed, and that if I wanted its company, I would call.

I sat alone for a long time, seeing the sky outside the window going from bright to dark. Finally, I made my decision. Maybe I'm an idealist; maybe idealism has no room left to breathe in today's society. But I still have my bottom line. I could accept the Hospice Dream Factory—but Mike's decision to expand the user base crossed my line.

The two founders had split over the company's direction—a split that, to the investors, looked ridiculous, hardly worth discussing. It boiled down to one sentence: here is a business that can make a fortune; it has a negative impact on consumers, though how much impact cannot yet be quantified; no law currently regulates it—do we do it or not? The facts proved that in the face of economic interest—especially the possibility of revenue growing by orders of magnitude—morality and conscience weren't worth mentioning. My reasoned arguments sounded weak and powerless.

"The risks you describe are hypotheticals with no data behind them. The opposite hypothesis is equally plausible—that the Dream Factory's benefits to society outweigh its harms." The VCs cut me off without even the patience to let me finish.

"We are not breaking any law." That was Mike's and the VCs' final answer.

The VCs all lined up behind Mike. So the choice before me was this: keep my head down and go along with it—change my views, stay at the company, share in the profits—or leave. For me, it was an easy choice—I'd already made up my mind before I ever sat down to negotiate with them.

"I figured you'd leave. I didn't think you'd give up your equity," Mike said to me. It was, perhaps, the last face-to-face conversation we would ever have.

"Compared to money, being able to examine my conscience each night and still fall asleep is worth more to me," I said evenly. "May you find peace." And with that, I walked away.

Saying those words, my feelings were tangled: a sincere blessing for Mike, a final warning to him, and perhaps, somewhere in my subconscious, a last thread of hope that his conscience might yet stop the expansion plan. But I would never truly know whether, deep down, Mike believed this VR Dream Factory would do more good than harm to the society or whether he had any pursuit beyond money at all. Perhaps only time could tell us what the VR Dream Factory would bring. And at that moment, however, due to different beliefs, Mike and I parted ways onto different roads. Whatever the reasons, whatever the motives—he chose his path, and I chose mine.
