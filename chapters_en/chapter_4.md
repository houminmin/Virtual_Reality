# Chapter 4: The Lake of Heaven

The motorcycle tore down the road, the mountain wind damp and cold; my nose, lips, and every inch of exposed face soon froze numb. There was only the vastness of sky and earth, the endless mountain road, and myself, hurtling into a world of green. Time seemed to have stopped in this world. Countless towering trees seemed to have stood here in silence for ten thousand years; the only things moving were the motorcycle beneath me and the wind it stirred up. I felt as if I were passing through a tunnel in time—the wind, the engine's roar, all seemed swallowed by the tunnel, leaving only this body of mine, still somehow in motion. Then it seemed I could no longer feel even the body. I was just thinking how strange this sensation was when a fresh sound broke into this world. What was it? I listened hard, and only after a long moment did it register: water!

The motorcycle stopped. It took me a while to recover the feeling in my hands and feet, and I heard Hari say "we'd rest here". I climbed off unsteadily and watched him walk into the trees, returning a moment later with a bottle of water, which he handed to me. "All natural, zero pollution!" he laughed. I took a drink—icy, sweet, bracing—and it hit my nerves like a thunderclap, jolting me alert. "Amazing!" I cried.

Hari was the guide I'd hired in Nation R. After walking away from the Dream Factory with nothing, the human simulation model experiments had lost their funding and had to stop. When I saw the news of the Dream Factory's new product launch, my chest tightened with frustration. So I decided: "Go! Go somewhere with no VR, no AI—no internet, even, no electricity!" So Ace searched and found this place in Nation R for me. By plane, train, coach, and minibus, after three days of travel I reached a small town deep in Nation R's interior and found Hari. Then Hari took me into the mountains by motorcycle, winding up and around—no asphalt or concrete, only rock and dirt, rough and narrow, too narrow for even the smallest car. Motorcycles were the only way through.

After three hours of jolting, a hidden paradise opened before my eyes. Snow-capped peaks, serene and remote against the blue sky. It was summer; meltwater ran down the mountainsides into a clear creek that sparkled crystalline in the sun. The creek threaded the valley between two great mountains—full and brimming in summer—and the valley was open and lush, wild flowers blazing everywhere. The valley was shaped like a gourd, connected to the outside world by a single narrow mouth. By the creek sat a small village with a few scattered households.

"Rest in the village tonight. We leave for the Lake of Heaven tomorrow." Hari settled me into a vacant little mud-brick house. "I have to go back to town to pick someone up." And with that he left. I sat in the doorway and watched the creek mirror the snow mountains as the sunset blazed, dyeing the peaks red, orange, and violet, the sky spread thick with pink and purple clouds.

---

The campfire burned high; the pot of water burbled and popped. Night had fallen. I sat on a rock resting my feet and lending Hari a hand. The first day's trek was over, and we had pitched camp in a clearing in the mountains. I had never walked so much mountain road in a single day; even with the trekking poles Hari gave me, I was wrecked. By now I was famished, and with the sun down the temperature was dropping fast, the cold closing in—I wanted nothing more than to lift the whole pot and drink the soup down in one go, hunger and cold solved together.

I'd never trained for mountaineering, and as the altitude climbed my body just couldn't keep up with my will. I hadn't thought much before setting out; only on the trail did I discover how hard the going was—no wonder even the locals born and raised in the village didn't venture up here. Fortunately Hari was experienced and had planned for me thoroughly in advance, which gave me the confidence to keep going.

"How are you holding up?" Hari turned to ask Woods, who sat silently to the side.

Woods took the soup Hari handed him, blew on it, sipped, and said slowly, "Not bad." Woods was the person Hari had gone to pick up—a middle-aged man, mid-forties by the look of him, wearing glasses, tall and thin, his hair touched with grey, the wrinkles around his eyes already deep. When we introduced ourselves before setting out, he said "you can call me Woods".

With hot soup and dry rations in our bellies, our spirits revived. The three of us sat around, chatting idly. I stretched out my legs, propped myself up on my elbows, and tilted my head back to look at the sky. The deep blue sky hung like an immense curtain, the stars clear and sharp, scattered across the whole of heaven. I could hear the burbling of the stream not far off, the crackle of branches burning down in the fire, the occasional coo of some bird. I was marveling at the vastness of the universe and the smallness of humankind, trying to recall the line that comes before "I could reach up and pluck the stars"—and realizing this poem of Li Bai didn't quite fit the scene anyway—when I suddenly heard Woods ask me: "Why did you come here?"

"Me?" I reeled my drifting thoughts back in and sat up. "Why did I come here?" I turned it over slowly. "Fate, maybe. I wanted to find somewhere quiet to clear my head, and this place happened to come up." Then I turned the question around and kicked the ball back to him: "Why did you come here?"

"Me?" Woods looked up at the sky. "I'd heard the Lake of Heaven is beautiful—too beautiful to belong to this world. So I wanted to see it."

---

I looked down and a wave of dizziness hit me. Below my feet the cliffs dropped sheer away, and the Lake of Heaven lay set into the mountain's mouth. Blue—so pure it might have been a tear fallen from heaven; how else could it be without a single flaw? Blue—so deep it seemed to hold some tremendous secret, gazing back and saying nothing. 

For a moment the wind stopped, and not one ripple crossed the lake. Smooth as a mirror, it reflected the sky and earth straight into the heart. The heaven above and the abyss below with the snow mountains in between rising and falling in majesty—time itself seemed to have frozen. 

I couldn't help spreading my arms, feeling myself become an eagle slicing through that frozen time and space, leaving behind one sharp cry. I imagined myself wheeling at high altitude, then suddenly folding into a dive, plunging into the water—and at the instant of entry becoming a great fish, swimming down toward the lake's depths. The water was ice-cold yet filled me with ease and joy, as if, wearied from flying the skies, returning to the water were a tired traveler finally home, sinking into a hot bath, body and soul utterly unwound. Somewhere in the deep blue there seemed to be a paradise. As I swam, a song drifted from nowhere, calling me to come and seek it, and full of curiosity and longing I pressed on—only to find the song forever out of reach, as if a mystery no mortal could approach.

Then through the song came a bird's cry that split the sky. I steadied myself: the mysterious song was Hari singing, and the cry was a real eagle passing overhead. I couldn't understand Hari's words; I only knew it soared like something not of this earth. I sat down and listened to the melody echoing through the mountains, weaving in and out of the wind, now clear, now blurred, like an echo from antiquity. My whole body trembled faintly with the force of it, and the tears just came. 

At some point the singing stopped. I wiped the tears from my face. Woods stood apart, alone, gazing at the lake, his expression unreadable from where I was. No one spoke; only the howl of the wind and the ripples on the lake gave proof that time and space had not frozen after all. After who knows how long, Hari spoke: "Time to head down." I stood, took one last long look at the Lake of Heaven, and fell in with the others, winding down the mountain in silence.

We camped in the mountains that night. After dinner by the fire, I noticed Woods packing up his cameras—several of them, large and small. I had brought no photography equipment at all this time. What the Lake of Heaven gave me was the shock and stirring of time, space, and life itself—something no camera can record, something that can only be received with the heart. Carved into one's heart, that kind of awe is never forgotten as long as one lives.

Hari asked, "So—this expedition, what has it stirred up in you two?"

What had it stirred up? I suddenly found myself thinking again of those recordings from the Hospice Dream Factory—the wealthy businessman who left the world with his "son" holding his hand, when what he'd wanted most, at the very end, was simply the time with his child that he himself had once given up; the old man who walked up to the stage again and again, stacking the applause higher and higher, never once managing, even at the end of his life, to let go of his hunger for fame. I used to watch those recordings and think the two of them were pitiful. Only now did I realize that I was, at bottom, no different from them—the world model, the human simulation model, the Dream Factory: yes, there was my love of technology in all of it, but there was also my own hunger for fame and fortune. I had fled Nation Q for Nation R because I still hadn't let go of my unwillingness to accept defeat, hadn't let go of equity that had multiplied several times over in value. But now the Lake of Heaven had struck my soul with a revelation: the universe is boundless, time runs long and far, and our brief lives are no more than specks of dust—and all the fame and fortune, all the endless striving after them, are smaller still, insubstantial as drifting smoke.

"The heavens are so vast, the mountains so magnificent," I said, "I suddenly understood how absurd, how laughable it is to spend a life chasing fame and fortune." All at once, I came to understand Zhuangzi's Great Roc—if it flew ninety thousand miles into the sky and looked down, surely all this scrambling and striving on the ground below would look absurd indeed.

Woods's hand, which had been poking at the fire, seemed to pause. But he smiled quickly and said only: "Too deep to fathom, too wonderful for words."

Hari didn't seem to understand what Woods and I said, only smiling at us. However, Woods's words reminded me of the song Hari had sung at the lake's edge, so I asked him: "What were you singing today?"

"Great God of Heaven, we thank you for making the springs sweet and never-failing; we thank you for making the forests lush, their treasures without end. The deep blue water of the lake is your eye, watching over us, telling us to love our parents and our brothers."

Such pure faith and gratitude—in today's world, how rare that has become.
