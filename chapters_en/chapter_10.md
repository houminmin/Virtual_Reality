# Chapter 10: The Loop

"Ace, if you gave Woods another chance, what do you think he would choose?" I stared out the window for a long moment before I spoke.

"He'd still choose to compromise with the government. Under those boundary conditions, it was the optimal choice." Ace was rational, unemotional as ever.

"But not the globally optimal one." I shook my head slightly.

"No one can predict the future. At the start, there was no way he could have seen what would come later." Ace shrugged.

"Right. Woods probably really did just love developing that fusion catalyst, in the beginning. He probably really did just want to use technology to benefit humanity." I let out a deep sigh.

"But one step at a time, he walked himself into a place where there was no choice left." There was something like regret in Ace's tone.

"Because of his own hunger for renown, and other people's hunger for profit." I paused. "Maybe that judgment is too harsh. I'm no exception myself—I can't escape greed, anger, and delusion any more than anyone else. It's always easier to say than to do."

"History is full of lessons," Ace said, with no mercy at all. "It's just a pity that the only lesson humanity has ever learned from history is that humanity learns nothing from history."

"Yeah." I thought of Mike, too—how he'd started out as a researcher who loved VR technology, and how, under the pull of enormous wealth, he'd ended up on a road with no way back. I still didn't know whether, in the end, he'd redeemed himself. But I preferred to believe he had.

"People who see through it all from the very beginning, the way Zhuangzi did—they're vanishingly rare." I sighed.

"Zhuangzi... which story do you mean?" Ace asked.

"Zhuangzi was once invited to take a high position at court, and he turned it down. He asked the messenger: do you think I'd rather be like the sacred tortoise, revered and enshrined at the temple after its death—or like the turtle in the pond, dragging its tail through the mud, free?"

"Wow. Zhuangzi really was something. Most people, offered a post like that, wouldn't dream of turning it down. But Zhuangzi refused." Ace sounded impressed.

"Right. When most people make a choice, all they see is what they stand to gain. But gain and loss are two sides of the same coin—they always come together. Wherever there's a gain, there's a loss, and wherever there's a loss, there's a gain. Zhuangzi saw clearly what he would lose if he took the high post—his freedom, maybe even his life. So, when he still had the choice, he chose the thing that mattered more to him."

"I really do admire Zhuangzi. That might be the highest wisdom a human being can reach." Ace sounded moved.

I froze for a second, staring at Ace. A robot, grasping the highest wisdom humanity had to offer—while most humans were still spinning blindly in the arena of fame and fortune.

---

I fell silent for a while, then decided to change the subject. "Why did Woods send those videos to me? Did he want me to use them as evidence to clear his name?"

"I don't think so. If he'd wanted to clear his name, he'd have sent them to a journalist, or to someone with real power and influence. You're an ordinary person—and the former CTO of the Dream Factory. As a witness, you wouldn't carry much credibility." Ace laid out the analysis, calm as ever.

I said nothing. Ace was right—maybe Woods had only wanted someone to know the truth. As for why he'd chosen me, I could only guess it was because of that brief stretch we'd walked together at the Lake of Heaven.

I refolded the letter, slid it back into the envelope with the chip, and locked the envelope in the bottom drawer.

Seeing that I had no intention of going to bed, Ace spoke again: "There's new progress in the human simulation experiment. Would you like to see?"

Right—nearly a month had passed since I went back to Nation R. So much had happened that I'd completely forgotten about my own experiment. The last time I'd checked, the virtual humans had discovered the laws of motion of their world. Who knew what stage their civilization had reached by now. I couldn't sleep anyway—better to do something than keep circling back, again and again, to what had happened to Mike and Woods.

Ace pulled up the latest experiment logs. The moment I saw them, I knew: tonight, of all nights, I would not find any peace.

Inside the virtual world I had made for them, the virtual humans had built a virtual world of their own.

A virtual virtual world.

"Even more interesting," Ace said, calling up the corresponding footage, "is that they've built something like a Dream Factory too. A great many virtual humans are drowning in it. And strong nations are using it to invade weak ones."

I went numb.

"Your hypothesis is nearly airtight now—the world we live in is very likely a virtual world created by a more advanced civilization." Ace seemed excited. "What will you do? Are you going to publish it?" It sounded as if Ace had already worked out how to write the paper.

I didn't answer. The night had grown deep; the city's glittering lights had dimmed. This was the quietest hour of the day. I stood up, walked to the window, and took several deep breaths, trying to put my thoughts in order.

"Ace," I said, gazing out the window, my voice drifting, "do you think humanity is ready for this hypothesis?"

"Hmm..." Ace seemed to not have expected that question, and it took a moment to think it through—perhaps because, to Ace, whether humanity was "ready" had no obvious bearing on whether I published the paper.

"This hypothesis was actually first proposed over two thousand years ago," I said, turning to look at Ace. "But humanity never took it seriously."

"What?" Ace sounded genuinely startled.

I smiled faintly. "Plato told a parable. He said there was a group of people living in a dark cave, bound in place, their eyes forced to look only forward, so all they could ever see were shadows cast on the cave wall in front of them. One of them was unusually curious. He broke free of his restraints and turned around—and discovered that the shadows were cast by puppets held up behind them, lit by firelight."

"Oh, I see!" Ace jumped in. "You're saying Plato of ancient Greece already said that the world humans see and feel is nothing but illusory shadows—which is the same thing as saying the world we live in is a virtual reality."

"That's right," I nodded. "But the real point of the parable comes after that. Once this man discovered the secret, he was thrilled, and he kept exploring the cave. He noticed a bit of light seeping in from somewhere. He followed it and found, faintly, an opening above him. He scrambled up and finally climbed out of the cave. Outside, the sunlight was so intense it nearly blinded him—but he discovered that outside, there were the real people and real things that the puppets inside had only been imitating."

"Oh! Those puppets are just like the virtual humans in our experiment!" Ace couldn't help exclaiming.

I smiled. "Yes and no."

Ace seemed to be working hard to figure out why I'd say that.

I paused, then went on. "After discovering the truth, this man was overjoyed, and he decided to go back down into the cave to tell his companions—to bring them out to see the real world outside too. But by then his eyes had adjusted to the light outside, and when he went back down into the darkness, he found he couldn't see a thing. The people in the cave listened to what he told them and didn't believe a word of it. Watching him stumble and trip, they laughed at him: 'You can barely even walk, and you want to teach us what's real and what's fake?' The man kept trying to persuade them—he genuinely wanted them to see the light of the real world—but the people in the cave didn't want to hear any more of it. So together, they stoned him to death."

Silence.

For a long time, Ace didn't speak. I couldn't tell whether it simply didn't have enough compute to spare for whatever it was thinking through. When Ace stayed quiet for that long, I got up and made myself a cup of coffee.

"Do you think there might be one or two virtual humans," Ace asked suddenly, "who, like the man in the parable who climbed out of the cave, discover that the world they live in is virtual?"

"Of course. Just as some humans have climbed out of the cave—Diogenes, whom I told you about before, and Zhuangzi and Plato, whom I mentioned today."

"And what happens to the virtual humans once they discover the truth?"

"What happens? Hmm... I don't know. But either way, they can't stop the experiment." I picked up my cup and took a sip of coffee. "But you know, Ace—there was a moment, just now, when I almost hit the emergency stop."

"Why?" This time Ace's response came quickly.

"Because I was scared. Maybe a week from now, maybe as soon as tomorrow, the virtual humans are going to act out humanity's own ending in advance. I was suddenly terrified of seeing what that simulated ending would look like. It felt like—how do I put it—like I was on a treadmill that had spun out of my control and just kept speeding up, and for my own safety, I had to slam the red emergency stop button, right now."

"But in the end, you didn't press it." Ace was looking at me steadily.

"No. I held back." I took another sip of coffee.

"Why did you hold back, in the end?"

"The future will come on its own—theirs, and ours. The virtual humans don't know their world is virtual. They still struggle in it, still create, still carry their days forward. And what we can do is the same."

Ace didn't say anything back.

I looked out the window. The first light of dawn was breaking. Day was almost here.
