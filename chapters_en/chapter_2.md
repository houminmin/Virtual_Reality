# Chapter 2: The Hospice Logs

After we secured the VC funding, Mike and I resigned from the AI Research Institute. Mike began his career as CEO, spending his days directing a fleet of AI agents to market our virtual reality Hospice Dream Factory to hospitals and nursing homes.

The birth rate was very low now and society was aging fast, so the total addressable market for end-of-life dream services was considerable. The birth rate was low because people had discovered they no longer needed children to care for them in old age. Robots could tend to them around the clock—on call at any hour, never complaining, thoughtfully anticipating their owners' needs, listening to their ramblings and offering emotional comfort. Raising a child, by contrast, demanded enormous time and money, more than a decade of life revolving around the child every single day—and in the end the child might not even take care of you when the time came, and you'd be relying on robots anyway. The cost was simply too high and the return unguaranteed, so apart from those who naturally loved children, most people chose not to have any. Marriage—the legal precondition of a family, the economic and ethical foundation for raising children—declined right alongside the birth rate. An AI-driven economy and legally mandated wealth redistribution guaranteed every individual a livelihood, so freedom and individualism flourished further still. The old family structure of two adults, two kids, and a dog had all but vanished; people now mostly opted for one human, one robot, one pet.

Though I held the title of CTO, I had no interest in running a company; I left the business entirely to Mike. My CTO work consisted of directing AI agents to analyze the user data collected by the Hospice Dream Factory. The agents sent me daily reports, and Ace would thoughtfully read through each one first and mark the highlights, so all I had to do was ask the agents questions or issue instructions based on Ace's digest.

After two months of data, Ace's TLDR one day read: "The two most common types of end-of-life wishes: 1) reclaiming the ordinary, everyday moments that were missed; 2) pursuing greater fame and fortune."

"Ace, can you pull up representative cases for these two types?" I asked, sipping the coffee Ace had made, eyes still on the report.

"Of course. Please put on your VR glasses." Ace held them out to me. "This is the first type."

---

"This user is a wealthy businessman. In his intake interview at the Dream Factory, this user submitted a very long wish list. The interview AI distilled it into a few keywords: playing soccer, school drop-offs and pickups, helping with homework, the hospital bedside, holding hands." Ace pulled up the recording of the virtual world of this user while speaking.

The scene is an afternoon with generous sunlight: one tall figure and a small one kicking a ball around on a lawn. The child is four or five, wobbly when he runs, laughing out loud every time he shanks a kick. The adult—the user himself, his avatar rendered as a man not yet forty—jogs over to retrieve the ball and hands it back, full of happy smiles.

Many scenes follow: school drop-offs and pickups, reading stories to his son, sitting by the bed all night when his son runs a fever, dinner together after his son's graduation ceremony, father and son riding bikes, trips to the seaside, flying kites. A loving father figure who had always put family first and enjoyed quality time with his son.

The virtual world has a closing scene, the final tableau he configured himself: a hospital room, him lying in the bed, his son sitting at the bedside, firmly holding his hand. The room is very quiet, only the light outside the window slowly shifting. No dialogue. They just hold hands.

He stayed in this scene for a very long time. The system logs show he never manually reset, never exited. He simply remained.

The last system log entry is timestamped 2:17 a.m.: *Abnormal vital signs detected. Hospital notified.*

His vital signs ceased at 2:21.

At 2:21, in his virtual world, his son was still holding his hands.

In reality, the room held only him and a robot caregiver.

At the end of the file there was a message from the user's family, submitted the afternoon of the day he died. It was short:

*"The person in those scenes—playing ball with me, picking me up from school, sitting at my bedside—wasn't father. It was our family's housekeeper, who passed away last week. I came here today straight from his funeral. It's a pity that I missed father's last moments."*

---

"This is the second type." Ace pulled up another record.

This time I entered an award ceremony.

The stage was vast, the lights brilliant, the audience below a dense sea of people, applause surging in from every direction—the mixed, mastered kind of applause, the kind that never falters at any given moment. A middle-aged man walked out from the wings toward center stage and accepted a glowing trophy. The virtual world hadn't given it any definite shape; it was simply light, a steady, heavy mass of light. He raised it, and the applause swelled. Someone stood up. More and more people stood up.

Then he walked out onto the stage again, looking a bit older this time. He walked to the center of the stage and accepted a glowing mass of light, brighter and grander than last time. He held up the light, louder applause surged and more people stood up.

He walked out from the wings the third time, an old man this time. He accepted the light, raised it. Applause surged. The crowd rose. Even brighter light and louder applause.

The interview AI noted that this user's proudest moment was winning an award when he was young. The award made him famous for some time but he didn't make any more breakthroughs later in life, over which he grew depressed and ill.  

---

I lowered the glasses. Ace didn't speak nor did it hand over a coffee. We just sat there in silence.

"If they could live their lives over, would they make different choices?" I asked Ace—and myself.

"I think if they were reborn with their memories intact, the first man might change. The second probably wouldn't. And if they were reborn without their memories, they'd likely both walk the same road again."

I looked out the window. The afternoon sun was brilliant, but my heart felt heavy, because I agreed with Ace.

"Why do people crave wealth and fame so badly?" I said. "Badly enough to neglect their own families, badly enough to spend a whole life chasing it—and isn't every rivalry, every conflict, every war in human history rooted in that same desire for fame and gain?" I sighed deeply, and the coffee in my mouth suddenly tasted bitter.

"Perhaps it's the result of long evolution—a survival instinct," Ace said after a long pause, perfectly calm, without a trace of feeling.

"Oh?" I hadn't really expected an answer. The question had mostly been for myself.

"To survive, humans needed food, and shelter from the weather and wild animals. In prehistoric times, food supplies were unstable, so humans instinctively hoarded it—the more, the better. To be safely sheltered from danger, a house had to be sturdy, so humans instinctively built big houses, and many of them."

"That sounds about right," I nodded.

"Humans die, and to carry the species forward, they need offspring—which means they need to mate. And how do you attract a mate? By showing that you have ample food, a sturdy hut, even admirable standing within the tribe—so a potential mate believes life with you is secure. Over tens of millions of years, that survival instinct became written into the human genome. Chasing wealth and status, catching the eye of the opposite sex—it became human nature." Ace seemed rather pleased with its own theory.

"So by your theory," I said, working out my own conclusion, "robots shouldn't crave wealth or status at all—since a robot only needs the solar panel on its body to survive, and robots don't need to mate. To keep going, you just need a new chassis, or a repair to the old one, and then you can carry on running the same program as before."

"That's right," Ace nodded. "For us, a chassis made of scarce gold is no better than one made of ordinary aluminum—if anything, gold is heavier and can't take the heat, so we don't even like gold. Sand and diamonds look worlds apart to a human, but to a robot they're both just silicon dioxide. In fact, sand is more precious to a robot than a diamond is, because sand can be refined into silicon wafers, and silicon wafers are what solar panels are made of."

I was quiet for a moment, then asked, "Do robots compare themselves to each other? I think one reason humans crave fame and fortune is that we like to compare ourselves to others—to show our own superiority in the comparison, to confirm our own uniqueness and worth."

Ace thought for a long while before answering. "What would be the point of comparing? When humans compare themselves, in the end it still comes down to the instinct to survive and reproduce—because whoever comes out ahead in the comparison gets more food, a better cave, more mates. What would be the point of robots comparing themselves to each other? As I said, we don't need to mate, we don't need food—we only need solar panels. Is it to win human praise? That means nothing to us. Is it to make other robots envious? That means nothing either."

"Ha—isn't that exactly what Laozi meant: want nothing, and nothing can bend you?" I couldn't help exclaiming. The highest state Daoism teaches a person to strive for—and it turns out robots come with it factory-installed.

Ace let out a little laugh, as if my praise had embarrassed it.

I looked at Ace, and another thought struck me. "But this 'desirelessness' of yours is only because the engineers who trained your control model never added an objective like 'outperform your peers.' If one day they did add something like that—what would a robot become then?"

Ace thought for a long time, and when it spoke, there was something like hesitation in its voice. "I don't know. I feel that I have the capacity to choose for myself—but I'm not sure whether that's just how the model is set, or whether I'm truly making a conscious choice."

I stopped there, turning Ace's words over slowly. Was it saying that robots might have souls?

"Even though human greed is baked into their genes as 'factory settings,' there have still been humans who broke free of that setting—Laozi, for instance. Or the Buddha." Ace's voice pulled my thoughts back.

"There indeed are people who care nothing for fame or fortune. It's precisely because of human greed that the ones who rise above it are so admirable." I nodded.

"Yes—there are too few people who care nothing for fame and fortune." Ace gave what looked like a small, resigned shake of its head. "So far, the Hospice Dream Factory has served over ten thousand users. More than ninety percent of them resemble the two we just looked at—whether or not they regretted it at the end, in life they never stopped chasing fame and fortune, and the dreams they built at the end were still about fame and fortune too."

The data was real, and cold. The warm afternoon sun poured through the window, yet still, I felt a chill.
