# Chapter 1: The Dream Factory

"You have an email from the Research Grant Committee of the National AI Research Institute..." It was six in the morning. Bleary-eyed, I stood at the sink, taking my cup and toothbrush from my robot, Ace, who launched right into the morning briefing.

"They regret to inform you that your research funding application has not been approved..."

"What?!" I was instantly awake. I work at the National AI Research Institute of Nation Q, where my research uses modeling and simulation to study the origins of humanity and its future development. The Institute's one-year startup funding had already run dry, and now the grant application had been rejected. Was the project going to die in its crib?

"Did they give a reason for the rejection?"

"'Too philosophical, no clear path to economic value.' In other words, they don't see the ROI," Ace said, perfectly even-keeled.

"Well, thank them for their candor!" Toothpaste foam sprayed all over the mirror.

"Would you like to send an email to express your gratitude?" Ace asked helpfully.

"Ha ha. Hilarious..." I shot Ace a glare. "NO! Get me Mike!"

Sensing my temper, Ace beat a hasty retreat and went to fetch my VR glasses.

Mike, my research partner at the Institute, had joined the same year I did. We met at the welcome party for new researchers. I'm an introvert, and I was standing there awkwardly, not knowing who to talk to, when Mike saw me alone and struck up a conversation. It turned out he wanted to study world models too. We talked up a storm, and by the end of the party we had a deal: pool our two startup funds and develop a more advanced world model together. The Institute gives its researchers maximum freedom but only one year of startup funding; after that, you have to win grants to keep your project going. After a year of relentless iteration—revamping the model architecture, the training methods, the data—our world model had finally become the new state of the art (SOTA). Not long ago, Mike and I had written a grant application to take the next step: developing a human simulation model. We assumed that, riding on our world model's success, the funding would be a breeze. Instead, we got clubbed over the head.

"Hey Mike, did you get the email?" I asked the moment the VR call connected. Mike looked like my call had dragged him straight out of bed. "Our project got rejected." I didn't bother checking whether he was awake enough, or braced enough, to face the cruel reality.

"WTF!" Mike swore. "Wait, let me check the email..." He raised his hand and swiped—pulling up the email in his VR glasses, presumably.

I signaled Ace to make me coffee, and off it went, cheerfully. Mike waved his hand again—done reading, it seemed. So I asked, "So, what's next?"

"We need to find money..." Mike heaved a long sigh.

He was right. Training models and running simulations both devour massive amounts of compute, which burns tons of cash.

"Find money where?"

"The entertainment market is on fire right now. Let's think about whether we can use our world model to make some money there." Mike leaned back in his chair, arms crossed.

AI had long since been deployed across every corner of production and daily life; mental and physical labor alike were broadly automated. The government redistributed wealth through taxes, providing citizens with a comfortable living allowance. The vast majority of people no longer needed to work and had oceans of free time, so they needed endlessly varied, ever-novel entertainment to kill it. World models can generate infinite images and video, so all kinds of world-model applications had long since flooded the entertainment industry.

"Won't the competition be brutal?" I took a sip of the coffee Ace brought over, composing myself. On the other end of the video, Mike was signaling his own robot to make coffee.

Mike rubbed his temples. "But our model is the most advanced, and it runs at one-tenth the cost of the previous SOTA. That alone makes us competitive."

True enough. Our world model could generate lifelike sound, imagery, touch, and smell simultaneously, in real time, extending indefinitely.

"What application, specifically? I want to restrict the user's control over the generated virtual reality—only expose control of the observation viewpoint. You and I both know that opening up too much control could be unsafe." I voiced the worry on my mind.

"Yeah, I know..." Mike scratched his head reflexively.

What we landed on was virtual reality tourism. The selling points: our world model's low running cost, plus a fully immersive experience—sound, light, smell, all utterly lifelike, like actually being there. With the application settled, Mike and I spent one day using AI agents to code and build the product, and then began the hunt for investment. 

Before we could address the real VC (venture capitalist), we had to convince the VC's robot assistants. We were failed by robot assistants a dozen-odd times. Some politely declined the moment they heard "world model." The rest listened to our pitch and gave us essentially the same feedback: not exciting enough, not enough drama. The public's senses had been numbed by every flavor of virtual reality on the market. You had to be novel—shocking, even—to stand out.

We finally passed the robot test at the 12th VC attempt, after which we were finally allowed to pitch to the VC himself. The investor was soft-spoken but razor-sharp in speech. He showed real interest in our model's low running cost, but told us bluntly that he didn't believe in the virtual tourism market, suggested we think up other applications, said we could talk again once we had one, and ended the VR call.

I took off my VR glasses and let out a heavy sigh. The sunlight outside was blinding; I couldn't help squinting.

"We have to give users more control over the virtual world. Otherwise it's too bland—it really won't attract anyone," Mike said frankly. He thought for a moment. "How about this: we build a virtual reality dream factory. Let users craft dreams in the virtual world—fulfill the wishes they can't realize in real life."

"Mike, you know that's risky. We haven't assessed the possible consequences. This dream factory you're describing could very well be Pandora's box." My tone turned serious. "We can't let capital hold us hostage—opening up the control mechanisms just to raise money. It could spiral out of control."

"We're using capital, not being held hostage by it. Without capital, there's no risk assessment for the world model, let alone development of the human simulation model. Other than fundraising, what choice do we have? Don't tell me you want to pass around a donation hat..."

I had to admit Mike was right.

"Besides, how do you assess the risks of a model without deploying it in the real world? This kills two birds with one stone: it funds the human simulation model, and it collects real data to evaluate the world model's risks. We can start with an alpha version, restrict the user base, and that limits the risk too." Mike's reasoning was solid.

"Restrict the user base... restrict it how?" I asked.

"Say—end-of-life care only. People at the end of their lives, carrying regrets. Their wishes come true in virtual reality—wouldn't that be a great kindness?" Mike said.

Perhaps it was the best compromise anyone could devise at this stage. I looked out the window and nodded. "Fine. But we disclose the risks. And we guarantee the research funding for the human simulation model."

The new application drew interest from several VCs. Through Mike's tireless lobbying (read: smooth talking), they agreed to let us funnel a large share of revenue into my research project—the one the Institute's grant committee had branded "too philosophical." Mike's justification: a human simulation model could better predict user needs, which would help us design better products, acquire and retain more users, and generate more revenue—hence excellent ROI, and so on and so forth.

Listening to Mike spin it, I grew deeply uneasy and shot him a look. He ignored me completely and kept right on talking.

"The point of the human simulation model is not predicting user needs... Why didn't you discuss it with me first?" I scolded him angrily afterward.

"You're too naive. What world do you think we're living in? Are VCs running charities? If we don't paint them a grand vision, why would they put in money?" Mike gave as good as he got.

I couldn't help but wonder—is it no longer possible to simply pursue a piece of science you find interesting?
