# Chapter 7: Reunion

Once the hypothesis—maybe we are living in a virtual world—had taken root in my mind, I suddenly missed the Lake of Heaven terribly. Its deep, fathomless blue was like a doorway to that ultimate reality. Hari's simple hymn was like sacred sound projected from that ultimate reality. Unable to suppress the longing burning in me, I prepared to set out at once and see the Lake of Heaven again.

While packing, I received a news alert: "Dream Factory IPO Succeeds; Founder's Net Worth Astronomical."

"Do you regret it?" Ace asked softly.

I smiled. "Ace, do you know the story of Alexander the Great and Diogenes? Alexander conquered half the Mediterranean. When he came to the conquered Greek city-states, he met Diogenes on the shore. Diogenes was a destitute philosopher in rags, sunbathing on the beach. Facing the most powerful, wealthiest monarch in the world, do you know how Diogenes reacted?"

Ace said: "Diogenes stayed where he was, lying at his ease in the sun, paying Alexander no attention. Alexander asked, 'Is there anything I can do for you?' And Diogenes said, 'Yes—step aside. You're blocking the sunlight.'"

"Alexander marveled: 'If I were not Alexander, I would wish to be Diogenes.' But Diogenes said: 'I certainly wouldn't wish to be Alexander.'" I smiled.

"I understand now," said Ace, and it seemed to smile too.

---

The plane landed in Nation R's capital. Leaving the airport, the streets were nearly empty of people—only a few police on patrol. Enormous concrete-and-steel buildings now towered along the roads; they hadn't been there on my last visit. I wondered privately what they were for, but I was in a hurry—the Lake of Heaven was still far off, with a train and a coach yet to catch—so I didn't stop to think. 

After a long, jolting journey I finally reached the little town outside the Lake of Heaven. The snow mountains shimmered in and out of view in the distance—but the town was deserted. Hari's little travel-agency shop was shuttered, the Lake of Heaven poster in the window the same one as before, now weathered and torn. What had happened here? An epidemic? A famine? There had been no word of war. Reaching the lake meant going into the mountains—hours by motorcycle, perhaps a day and a night on foot—and I didn't know the way. What was I to do? The streets were empty of any human trace. Bewilderment, frustration and fear crashed over me at once; I felt clubbed over the head, trembling all over, and could only sink down at a street corner to collect myself. I tried to contact Ace, but the town had no signal.

Night fell, and the distant snow mountains sank into darkness. Under the assault of hunger and cold, my burning longing for the Lake of Heaven curdled into enormous pain and disappointment. I huddled into myself. Just as despair was closing in, a beam of light swept up the street. A car! I rushed toward it.

The car braked hard. The driver leaned out—it was Woods.

We both froze. "What are you doing here?" we asked each other at the same instant.

"What happened here?" I pressed. "Are you coming from the Lake of Heaven?"

Woods didn't answer. He only said: "Get in. I'll take you to the train station. The Lake of Heaven is out of reach now—you know this car can't handle the mountain roads."

I wanted desperately to go to the lake, but reality being what it was, I could only yield. Woods said not a word the whole way. I asked "what happened?" several times; he just shook his head, and I gave up.

We reached the station deep in the night. The instant I stepped out and was about to shut the door, Woods suddenly said, "Leave Nation R now—go back to Nation Q." Then, without waiting for me to say a word, he gunned the engine and sped away. I watched the taillights vanish into the dark, my mind thick with foreboding.

---

Back in Nation R's capital, driven by curiosity, I walked up to one of the concrete towers to see for myself. Several fully armed soldiers stood at the entrance—and when they saw me approach, they stopped me.

"Who are you? What's your business?" the soldier snarled.

My mind raced. The soldiers' uniforms bore Nation Q's national emblem—clearly Q had sent them—so my Q citizenship should still serve as a talisman. But what was my business? Tourism? A place guarded by soldiers wouldn't admit ordinary tourists. Journalist wouldn't work either—whatever was here looked classified, hence the garrison. That left only one option: claim the Q government sent me.

"I'm a special commissioner from the Q government, here on an inspection." My insides quailed, but my face had to hold.

The soldier looked me up and down, plainly unconvinced. "Credentials?" he said. I dug my passport out of my bag and handed it over; he was obviously still waiting for a work badge or the like.

Just as my lie was about to collapse, a voice behind me said: "She's with me."

I turned—and it was Mike. The instant the soldier saw him his expression changed; he clearly knew Mike, knew him well. Mike stepped forward and exchanged a few words, and the soldier dropped the matter of credentials.

Mike came up to me, his voice calm, as though our parting had been only yesterday: "Let's go up and have a look first." I was too stunned to speak; I just nodded mechanically and followed him into the elevator, up to the top floor.

The doors opened onto what looked like a control room. Through the glass curtain wall, the floor below was visible. Row upon row of seats, packed tight, a person in every one—belted at the waist, VR glasses and headphones on, a drinking tube at the mouth. They twisted and gestured as if puppeted by some ghost, their postures ugly and absurd. Every so often someone collapsed, and the soldiers standing watch would walk over, unstrap the fallen from the seat, lay them on a stretcher, and carry them off to who knew where.

In an instant I understood what these concrete buildings were for. Nation Q was using the VR Dream Factory to imprison and control the people of Nation R. But why? Why would Q's government commit such a crime against another country while protecting its own citizens with a VR Safety Act at home? Why had Nation R met this catastrophe? Were there other countries too? And why was Mike doing this work for the government?

I glared at Mike, so furious I didn't know where to begin. Mike didn't seem inclined to explain. He watched the big screen, careful not to meet my eyes.

Suddenly a photo flashed onto the screen, beside it a glaring red exclamation mark and the words "LIFE-THREATENING." I recognized the face at once: Hari. I looked down at the floor below—someone was about to be carried out. "Is that the person being carried away?" I asked Mike, pointing at the screen.

Mike nodded.

"Where will he be taken?" I asked.

"The emergency room."

"Take me there. Please."

Mike hesitated a moment, then rose and led the way.

"How many people are in this building?" I asked as we walked, fighting to keep my voice level.

"Ten floors. Ten thousand per floor."

It took everything I had not to cry out.

---

In the emergency room, Hari lay on the bed, unconscious. Medical staff worked on him with expressionless faces. I watched him through the glass, refusing to believe what I was seeing. A year ago Hari had been so strong, so radiant—a man who could carry a hundred-pound pack and still move light as a swallow, joking and laughing as he climbed. The hymn he sang at the Lake of Heaven had been bold and tender, like a sound not of this earth. Now he lay here, his life hanging by a thread, skeletal, silent.

A doctor and several nurses emerged from the next room over, one of them shaking his head and sighing: "What's the point of saving these walking corpses? Revive them, send them back into VR immersion, and they're carried in again—isn't this just a waste of medical resources?" The moment they noticed me—a stranger—they fell silent.

"I have to take this man out of here," I said to Mike. "Whatever it takes, I'm taking him."

"No one from Nation R leaves this place. Except the dead."

"Doing something this inhuman—can your conscience bear it?" I had finally found my first question.

"How can an individual fight the machinery of the state?" Mike shot back.

"An individual can at least refuse to board the ship!"

"You don't understand..." Mike started, then stopped.

I was silent for a moment. Then I said: "Do you know the name of the man in there? His name is Hari. A year ago he took me to the Lake of Heaven."

Mike didn't react.

"Have you ever been to the Lake of Heaven?" I asked, my voice slowing. "The blue there—it isn't a color this world should have." I paused. "It woke me up. A life that doesn't pursue truth and goodness, that only scurries after rank and wealth, is a life walking on drifting clouds—dazzling and busy on the surface, but hollow through and through. And how absurd, how laughable, such a life is."

Still Mike didn't react.

I thought in silence for a long moment before I spoke: "You understand the VR technology. If you were willing, you could save these people."

Mike never answered. He only watched the medical staff at their hurried work inside the emergency room.

---

In the end, I could not save Hari. With soldiers patrolling everywhere, no one to help me, and Hari unconscious, there was no way I could take him out. The truth is, I understood: just bringing me into the building, Mike had already run an enormous risk. Without him, I could never have entered and left those concrete walls safely.

The moment I walked out of the concrete building, I called Caroline. The line rang for a long time before she finally picked up.

"If a Q-based company's VR product, when deployed to users abroad, seriously exceeds the safety threshold—does that violate the VR Safety Act? Can we bring a legal case against a Q company that does this?" I stared at the screen, fighting to hold back the grief and fury boiling inside me.

Caroline didn't answer right away. She picked up her water glass and took a sip, as if choosing her words carefully. "Someone once said politics is the art of compromise," she said slowly, not answering my question directly. "The law is no different."

"What do you mean?" My eyebrows shot up; something inside me lurched.

"At the time, our VR Safety Act ran into fierce resistance. In the end, to get it passed, we had to make certain compromises—we could only guarantee safety for people at home first..." Caroline's voice had lost its usual bluntness and precision.

"So," I said—I already understood what she wasn't saying—"the VR Safety Act was written with a loophole left in it, so the VR providers could go make their money overseas. That's what got them to stop blocking the bill. That's how our VR Safety Act actually passed. Is that it?" All that was left in me was a deep, hollow helplessness.

Caroline didn't answer.

I hung up quietly. I didn't blame Caroline, not really. She had been clear-eyed the whole way through—clear-eyed even while making the compromise. She understood exactly what that compromise meant, and it left her with no good options either. She had weighed it and chosen the side with a little more benefit and a little less harm. At the very least, her efforts had won real protections for some people, and that was no small thing. Maybe a compromise like that brings more sunlight into the world than stubbornly insisting the sun should shine on everyone, everywhere, all at once.

On the flight back to Nation Q I thought for a long time about whether to make public what I had seen in Nation R. What would exposing the truth cost? Detention, perhaps. Perhaps a quiet, traceless death. I will not pretend I felt no fear. Facing a choice with my life in the balance, all those righteous words I had flung at Mike—"the meaning of life is the pursuit of truth and goodness"—suddenly stood on shaking legs. I understood, abruptly, that between awakening and practice there lies a deep and bitter chasm.

For days I struggled in anguish, sitting alone by the window from sunrise to sunset, drafting the post and deleting it, over and over. I asked Ace what I should do—protect myself in prudent silence, or fly like a moth into the flame. Ace said nothing. I knew: Ace could not give me the answer. No one could.
