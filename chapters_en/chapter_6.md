# Chapter 6: Awakening

After the VR Safety Act passed, the clamor on social media receded without a trace, as if nothing had ever happened. The human simulation model experiments now had the national research fund behind them—for this year, at least, I had nothing to worry about. During the legislative push, the experiments' direction had shifted toward producing simulation data for VR safety thresholds, but with the act now passed, I could return to my original research: observing the evolution of virtual humans. All was calm.

In my experiment, I used the world model that Mike and I had trained together to construct a lifelike virtual world, then placed one million virtual humans inside it. These virtual humans were driven by human simulation models, each with different initial conditions. The models were trained in the virtual world through reinforcement learning, continually updating their parameters, so the virtual humans continually evolved. The heart of reinforcement learning is setting an objective, and the objective I gave these virtual humans was:

Live a long and happy life.

Simple on its face, but with depth beneath. This objective is humanity's universal wish, which is why I used it to approximate human evolution. But while "long" can be quantified, "happy" resists quantification—a thousand people, a thousand definitions. More importantly, happiness need not be determined by environment; it need not come from external rewards; it can come from an internal reward mechanism. And I had given the virtual humans no internal reward mechanism at all—only external, quantifiable ones. So I wanted to observe how the virtual humans would define happiness, and whether they could build an internal system of values—what I defined as a "soul."

When I explained my experimental design to Ace, it raised serious doubts. When Ace worried the experiment might fail, I just laughed: "Let's just let it run for a while and watch. The Institute just doubled my compute quota anyway—if this run fails, we can start over from scratch."

The Institute had sent a notice a few days earlier granting all researchers double compute. No reason given, but the news made it easy to guess. A new type of nuclear plant had recently come online, using a novel catalyst to trigger fusion; plant efficiency and output had jumped, AI compute costs had halved, and so on the same budget the Institute received twice the compute—and passed the windfall on to every researcher.

Once the experiment was running, I handed the task of logging and organizing the data to Ace, packed my bags, and went camping.

---

"You need to see the experiment logs from the past few days," Ace said. I was just back from camping, fresh from the shower, eating a sandwich Ace had made. For Ace to be this impatient—not even letting me finish eating—the data had to be interesting. Ace had probably been restraining itself for a while, holding back from showing me the data the moment I walked in.

"Sure," I mumbled through a full mouth. I could almost see the excitement on Ace's face—though its face is metal, incapable of any expression.

I devoured the rest of the meal and put on my VR glasses. What appeared was an experiment segment Ace had frozen.

The virtual humans had invented writing.

At first there were just symbols, used to mark quantities of food and ownership. Then, as the timeline rolled forward, the symbols evolved into the rudiments of writing—script for recording contracts of trade.

I replayed it over and over, unable to believe the logs in front of me. No one had taught the virtual humans what writing was or what it was for. They had created it themselves, through interaction with their environment and with one another.

"Now look at this segment." Ace could barely contain the excitement in its voice. "One of the virtual humans has written down laws resembling Newton's three laws of motion—they just gave the laws different names."

The virtual humans had built their own civilization—and this virtual civilization bore a striking resemblance to humanity's.

"Your hypothesis is confirmed! We indeed can use human simulation models, in a virtual world generated with a world model, trained through reinforcement learning, to approximately reproduce the developmental laws of intelligent life—and from there, infer humanity's origins and future." Ace raised its hand for a celebratory high five.

But I had sunk into thought.

Ace stood there with its hand up, waited a long time, then slowly lowered it.

I didn't speak. I stared at those symbols on the screen—writing that had grown by itself—and felt something shifting slowly through my mind, something I hadn't yet caught hold of.

A few minutes passed, maybe longer, before I spoke.

"Ace," my voice was lower than usual, "have you ever wondered—these virtual humans... do they know they're living in a virtual world?"

"They don't," Ace said. "Their senses can only perceive the world that we constructed for them. That world, to them, is the whole of reality."

"Right," I said. "Then what about us? Maybe we're living in a virtual world too. Maybe our world is a virtual reality created by a more advanced civilization."

Silence.

I didn't look for Ace's reaction; I kept going, half talking to myself. "First: we cannot refute this conjecture—that is, we cannot prove we're not living in a virtual reality. All our observations of our world and universe come through our senses, and our senses are limited; we can only observe and feel what we are able to observe and feel. Just like the virtual humans created in the experiment: they come to know their world through their senses—through the interaction protocols I pre-defined between the human simulation models and the world model. The virtual world built by that world model seems utterly real to them. In it they are born and they die, they gain and they lose; everything seems solid and true. They struggle and explore inside it, trying to understand and change this world—virtual to us, real to them—trying to take hold of their fate. How is that any different from us? How can we say with certainty that the world we know isn't virtual?"

"We truly cannot refute that conjecture," Ace said—not interrupting my monologue, only summarizing when I paused.

"There's something else I've been turning over for a long time." I pulled up Ace's summary of the experimental observations again. "What you wrote here—the virtual humans tend to maximize their own short-term gain, their rationality is bounded, their compassion is thin—"

"Yes," Ace said. "Compared to humans, they're less perfect."

"Less perfect." I repeated the words. "That's exactly my point. The human simulation model was designed to approximate humans, but through the model mapping, the virtual humans came out less perfect than humans. So—if we, too, are a mapping made by some higher civilization—following the same logic upward—the civilization that created us should be more perfect than we are. One more level up: more perfect still. Trace it all the way to the root, and there you should find pure goodness."

"But that inference rests on a premise," Ace said. "That every mapping loses some perfection. Does that itself hold? Perhaps a mapping can be lossless."

I thought for a moment. "Lossless mappings exist in theory. But in the experiment, what we actually observed was lossy—the virtual humans are less perfect than humans. Maybe that lossiness isn't an accident but the price of creation itself. To create is to separate something out from the perfect—and what is separated is no longer whole. There is always loss."

Ace was quiet for a while and said nothing more.

"And another thing: the timescale inside the experiment's virtual world is compressed relative to ours, because I need the experiment to finish quickly—I'm using roughly one year of our time to simulate tens of thousands, even hundreds of millions of years of their history."

"You're saying that as we trace back toward the root, the timescale keeps expanding—so the final root would have an infinite timescale. Which is to say: eternity." Ace picked up the thread.

I looked at Ace and smiled. "So," I drew a deep breath, "the root derived from the hypothesis that our world is a virtual reality is the same as Aristotle's First Unmoved Mover, the God of Christianity, the Dao of ancient Chinese Daoism. Whether by philosophy or religion, by rational inference or revealed faith, the conclusion matches ours: that root is perfect and eternal. Only that root is reality—and every other world, including the one we live in, is virtual."

"Then why would that perfect, eternal root create so many imperfect virtual worlds?" Ace asked.

I froze for a second; I hadn't expected so sharp a question. "That is a question humanity can never answer. Because we are only small, momentary virtual realities—we can never perceive, never comprehend, never imagine that eternal, perfect reality. We can only know that it exists, because if it did not, we could not. Perhaps all virtual realities are nothing more than a dream that reality is having." I said it slowly.

"If your hypothesis is true—if humans really live inside a virtual reality—then what is the meaning of life? Why live at all?" Ace seemed to have shifted its critical thinking into top gear.

A hard question. I had thought about a similar question at the Lake of Heaven. So it didn't take me long to come up with an answer.

"The meaning lies in awakening—and then, after awakening, in transcending. If we are all virtual beings, and everything in this world, fame and fortune included, is virtual, then we should awaken to the fact that chasing fame and fortune is meaningless—that competing and even slaughtering one another over virtual gains is absurd. Instead we should transcend the parade of phenomena in this virtual world, transcend the limits of our own virtual existence, and strive toward that reality of perfect truth and perfect goodness."

"You seem to be saying humans should transcend human nature?" Ace's tone was pure incredulity.

"Transcend material desire, fame, and fortune. Make life simple and motives pure; seek truth, seek goodness. When a person stops depending on feedback from their environment and looks inward instead—measuring the happiness dimension of reward against their own internal system of values—they can reach the goal of living a happy life independent of their circumstances. They transcend their environment. They gain the greatest freedom there is."

"That is what you meant by save our souls." Ace looked at me as it said it.

I looked back at Ace and said nothing.

I thought of all the posts tagged #SaveOurSouls—hundreds of thousands of them, millions. Every person who reposted it believed they understood. And here was this robot with a metal face incapable of expression, who had said, in a single sentence, the thing my post had truly been trying to say.

"Yes," I said softly, at last. "That is save our souls."
