# Chapter 5: Legislation

Mike's ability to execute was, I had to admit, impressive. By the time I returned from Nation R, the Virtual Reality Dream Factory had swept the nation. Perhaps the Dream Factory really was riding the current of the times. AI had taken over production, people no longer needed to work—so what was there to do with each day? Learning, thinking, discovery, creation were strenuous, and in a fully AI-run era seemed to have lost their point; hedonism—the pursuit of sensory thrill and pleasure—became the default choice. But pleasure demands constant novelty, or boredom and weariness set in fast. The Dream Factory could cheaply create thousands upon thousands of distinct virtual worlds, feeding the senses an endless stream of fresh stimulation. If you could imagine it, you could experience it in the Dream Factory. Inside it, a person became "God"—and how many people can resist the temptation of becoming God? How many can resist the pleasure of desires easily satisfied, even if it's all virtual?

I felt I had to speak. The deepness of the Lake of Heaven and the grandeur of the snow mountains seemed to have filled me with strength. I was convinced that everyone had the right to know the risks the Dream Factory carried. Maybe Mike had been right—if he didn't build the Dream Factory, someone else would. The Dream Factory was a product of its era; the technology behind it was not the problem. The risk in its application came from human weakness.

One night, I finally wrote down my cry:

"In an age of material abundance beyond anything the world has ever seen, are human beings happier than we used to be? No. We are more miserable than ever—rates of depression and suicide have reached historic highs. We try to use technology to build a counterfeit happiness, to soothe a hollowed-out soul. But can we really find comfort for our souls in a virtual world? Human desire is a bottomless pit that can never be filled. When desire goes unmet, we suffer. When it is met, that flash of pleasure is instantly swallowed by boredom. To be worn down in an endless loop between suffering and boredom—can that be called living? SOS! Save our souls! Rescue your soul from the virtual world! Seek truth, do good, behold beauty, and joy will rise from within on its own. Once the soul is free, how easy it is to live a happy life—all you need is enough food to fill your stomach, and a simple bed!"

Of course—predictably, and disappointingly—my cry was a handful of sand dropped into the sea. It raised not a ripple. I was like a caged beast, full of strength with nowhere to spend it; the only consolation left to me was the blue of the Lake of Heaven. Just when I thought God had shut every door and window on me—ding—a reply appeared under my post.

"The spiritual awakening and self-discipline you call for—only a tiny minority can achieve them. For the vast majority, only law can regulate and restrain. I am drafting a bill to regulate VR product providers. Would you be interested in serving as my advisor?" Signed: Caroline. Ace quickly looked her up: this Caroline was a member of the national House of Representatives.

It seemed God had left me a window after all. "Of course," I replied.

The video call invitation rang almost instantly. I put on my VR glasses and answered, and a poised, mature woman appeared before me.

"I'm glad you're willing to take part in the legislation. You're a co-founder of the virtual reality Dream Factory and an expert in VR technology. And you advocated restricting the Dream Factory's user base while the risks remained unknown—which aligns closely with the VR safety bill I intend to introduce. So I'm confident we'll work well together." Caroline went straight to the point. No wasted words.

"I agree that law should be what regulates public behavior. You're right—expecting everyone to rely on personal awakening and self-discipline isn't realistic."

"I believe the problem must be solved at the source. My approach: from now on, every virtual reality product must undergo safety testing, and the law will define the standards and procedures for that testing, along with quantitative metrics for safety ratings," Caroline said.

"I agree with the framework. But the testing standards and procedures, the quantitative safety metrics—none of that can be conjured out of thin air. It needs data. Collecting data costs money and time. Where do those resources come from?" I voiced my biggest worry.

"We start with conservative, strict rules. Over-caution beats losing lives. Legislation is a long process—we put the bill out first, get it into the pipeline, and amend it along the way as the data comes in."

I thought for a long while, then said slowly: "Then we should start the legislative process immediately. As for the supporting data—I used to do research on human simulation models. My idea is to run simulated tests with virtual humans instead of sampling real people. Far cheaper, far safer, and it would compress the data collection timeline enormously. The only problem is that the research was halted for lack of funding." I couldn't help a self-mocking little smile.

Caroline's eyebrows shot up. "Wait—you didn't apply for the Institute's research grant?"

"I did, but was rejected." I sighed.

"Why?" Caroline sounded like she couldn't believe it.

"They thought my project 'too philosophical'." I gave a resigned shrug.

"Mm. Your human simulation model is no longer philosophical—it's critical." Caroline's brow creased slightly. "Reframe its application around helping set VR safety standards, then apply again. I'll write you a recommendation letter."

"Done!" My fighting spirit was back.

---

This time my research funding was approved—Caroline had surely worked the Institute's grant committee hard behind the scenes. But the legislative road was tortuous. The entrenched interests of the VR business were deep-pocketed conglomerates; "we are not breaking any law" had been their most powerful argument, and they would spend whatever it took to keep that argument true. The VR lobby's campaign in the legislature threw up enormous resistance to our bill. The VR Safety Act looked set to die in its cradle—until the turning point arrived in a way no one expected.

It began with a post that drew massive attention on social media, signed "A Repentant Mother Who Lost Her Child": "My son Jack was killed by the Dream Factory. He was only sixteen. #TheDreamFactoryIsADementor #GiveMeBackMySon #DownWithTheDreamFactory." The post included photos and videos of Jack—a sunny, handsome boy. By his mother's account, Jack had been on his school's basketball team, until the Dream Factory "sucked out his soul": he went days without eating or sleeping and died right there at home. And the mother had noticed nothing—because the Dream Factory had sucked out her soul, too.

Reading the post, a chill ran down my spine. Expected, and yet unexpected. Expected, because I had known the Dream Factory would end in disaster—that fear was exactly why I'd opposed Mike's expansion. Unexpected, because I never imagined it would happen this fast. Just then the video call chime sounded. It was Caroline.

"Have you seen the post about Jack? More cases are surfacing—comas, shock, even deaths. And multiple crimes: the suspects all testify that after using the Dream Factory they couldn't tell reality from the virtual world—they thought they were inside the Dream Factory, where anything goes." Caroline was direct as ever. "This is an extremely favorable opening for our bill, and we have to use it well. I'm going to dig up your old 'save our souls' post and use it to build momentum for the VR Safety Act."

Soon "#VRSafetyLegislation #SaveOurSouls #SOS" was spreading across social media, and the long-suppressed VR Safety Act stepped into the spotlight. In Caroline's telling, she and I had seen the Dream Factory's danger long ago—I had posted my opposition, she had introduced her safety bill—but the VR lobby had used capital to manipulate the legislature and obstruct it, and now people were dead; the Dream Factory owed these victims an answer. Caroline's post read: "Other people's health, dignity, even lives are worth nothing in the money games these corporations play. So long as they can lounge in their mansions flaunting their ever-growing fortunes, they could not care less whether that wealth was won by harming others. Conscience? Conscience has no seat at the table in the VR lobby's game." She also posted a screenshot of the Dream Factory spokesperson's statement: "We merely provide a product for consumers to choose. If a consumer buys an appliance and burns their house down through improper use, do we blame the manufacturer? The responsibility for misuse lies with the consumer, not the producer."

One stone raised a thousand waves. Posts denouncing the Dream Factory blanketed every platform. Some people even published videos of virtual worlds they had built inside the Dream Factory—worlds in which the Dream Factory's offices were destroyed and its investors and executives beaten. Using VR to protest VR - I didn't know whether to laugh or cry. Public fury boiled, and events seemed on the verge of slipping out of control. People actually pulled themselves away from their pleasures and took to the streets, demonstrating outside the Dream Factory's headquarters and the legislature. In the end, under the weight of public opinion, the legislature passed the VR Safety Act. The Dream Factory announced it was suspending its dream-making service for the general public, keeping only the end-of-life care service.

The day the bill passed, Caroline called me, smiling: "We can finally pop the champagne. With the law in place, we can rest easy."

I put down the call, but my heart felt neither light nor glad. I stood alone facing the window, thoughts churning, for a long time—until at last a thread emerged.

"Ace," I called.

Ace appeared quietly at my side and handed me a glass of water.

I took it, sipped, and said slowly: "As I see it, the soul is a force that rises from within—the power to choose for itself, a force of inner value that doesn't depend on external rewards. When I wrote 'save our souls,' I was calling on people to discover that inner force, to train it, to use it—to choose joy that doesn't come from VR's manufactured satisfactions. But the Dream Factory's victims walked in and drowned in its virtual worlds."

Ace didn't interject. It seemed to be waiting for me to finish the argument.

"The victims denounce the Dream Factory as a Dementor—forgetting that Harry Potter defeated the Dementors by his own will, casting 'Expecto Patronum' to summon a protector. And his Patronus was himself," I went on.

"Harry Potter also went through grueling training to forge a spiritual core that strong. Not everyone can do it," Ace said.

I nodded. "Yes. That's the weakness in human nature—looking inward for strength has always been one of the hardest things for human beings. The Dream Factory's rebuttal looks airtight, but it demands that everyone be a fully trained Harry Potter, while it lets the Dementors of its own making roam the world without restraint."

"The Dementors are powerful and the human will is fragile—so the Dementors must be locked away," Ace picked up the thread.

"But as virtue climbs a foot, the devil climbs ten. The Dementors will find every possible way out of Azkaban. Law is only the most basic restraint; the real solution is for every person to train into a Harry Potter who can cast a Patronus. And yet we have to admit that what Caroline said is the reality—'expecting the majority to rely on their own spiritual awakening and self-discipline is impossible.' Now #SaveOurSouls is on everyone's lips, and no one understands what it actually means." I sighed.

Outside the window stretched a cloudless sky, yet I seemed to see wisps of dark cloud gathering at the horizon. This might be only the beginning.
