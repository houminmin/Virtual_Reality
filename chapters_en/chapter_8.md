# Chapter 8: The Scapegoat

Finally, after several sleepless nights in a row, in the small hours of one morning I wrote the post about Nation R and clicked send. I sat alone in the stillness, as if all the strength had been drained from my body. My feelings in that moment were tangled: I knew I had done what had to be done, and that knowledge brought ease and relief; at the same time I had no idea what awaited me, and that brought fear and dread. The sky brightened little by little; the early birds had begun to sing. Whatever happens, I told myself, I have kept my soul.

For days, nothing happened. My post drew no attention at all. The people of Nation Q seemed to have other, more important things to attend to—Nation R, thousands of miles away? Where even is that, and what does whatever happened there have to do with us? Some sports star is caught in a scandal—gotta keep up with that; some pop singer just announced a concert—gotta hurry and grab tickets—no time, no time at all. As for the Q government, it seemed equally indifferent to my unnoticed little post. I had to laugh at myself: this was the reception of the momentous decision I had agonized over for so long. And I grieved, helpless—were those innocent people simply to suffer in silence, unseen?

I was racking my brain over what to do next to save the people of Nation R when Ace suddenly handed me my VR glasses. "Major news," it said. "Look, quickly."

I put them on. The footage looked covertly shot, but it was unmistakably inside one of those concrete towers in Nation R. People in the seats were struggling. Someone pulled off their VR glasses and stared blankly around, like a person surfacing from deep water. Then more people stood; restraint belts snapped; some fell; some helped others up; some bolted straight for the doors. Q soldiers were swinging batons. Some people shrank back.

I took the glasses off. Put them on again. Took them off.

Was this real?

My head was chaos, emotions surging over one another. It looked as if the VR system controlling the people of Nation R had failed. Had Mike sabotaged it from the control room—or was it a real malfunction? Either way, Mike would surely be held to account by the Q government this time. And who had filmed the videos? Was it Mike?

The leaked footage was reposted and dissected across the internet at blinding speed. Soon, journalists from every country poured into Nation R. Masses of on-the-ground videos went up online, and the whole world learned of the Q government's despicable acts in Nation R. Those who had escaped the concrete buildings told the story from the beginning: the Q government had first gathered the people of Nation R into training camps under the banner of "training R citizens in VR technology to advance R's technological development." But once the people put on the VR glasses, they were hooked as if drugged, unable to stop. Later the Q government used outright force to keep them immersed; the few who occasionally surfaced and struggled were suppressed. The world erupted. The Q government's carefully cultivated image as a champion of freedom and democracy collapsed; public opinion turned on it as one. Calls for the president to step down grew louder and louder inside Nation Q. At home and abroad, everyone demanded an explanation: why had the Q government carried out so inhuman an invasion of Nation R?

A few days later, the Q government finally answered.

The government's statement was long, its wording exhaustively careful, going in circles. Only at the third paragraph did the gist come clear: the government claimed everything stemmed from the personal recommendation and private dealings of a certain scientific advisor.

At the end of the statement were a photo and a name.

I read the name first; it meant nothing to me. Then I looked at the photo and froze.

It was Woods.

His true identity: Nation Q's National Chief Scientist and strategic advisor to the President.

The government explained that Woods had reported to them that deep within Nation R's Lake of Heaven lay a critical strategic resource—a substance that could be used to synthesize a new type of catalyst, one that would dramatically improve the efficiency of nuclear fusion and the output of power plants. To secure this resource, and to keep its reserves and uses hidden from other nations, Woods had advised the government to place the people of Nation R under mental control. At that same moment, the Dream Factory's executive happened to be lobbying the government to push its product into international markets, paving the way for the IPO. Woods and the Dream Factory struck a private deal, and so he championed the Dream Factory's VR technology to the government as an instrument of mental colonization. Woods was now in custody awaiting trial, charged with "accepting bribes" and "crimes against humanity."

By this communiqué's telling, the Q government had done nothing wrong; every fault lay with Woods alone. It seemed to hold together on the surface, but a moment's thought exposed the gaping hole: Woods had only advised and advocated. The final decisions were never his to make—yet now the government heaped all responsibility onto the man who had given the advice. If you wish to condemn a man, you will never lack for a charge.

I got up and walked out, driven by some force I couldn't name to find Mike. If Woods was already in custody, what would they do to Mike? The communiqué's "Dream Factory executive" plainly meant him. I didn't know why I was going—maybe because of the way Mike had started to speak and stopped himself that day in Nation R; maybe because, whatever else, he had once been my colleague and my friend.

I arrived at Mike's mansion and rang the bell. After a long while the door finally opened—Mike's robot butler.

"Is Mike in?" I asked.

The robot butler seemed to recognize me; it didn't ask who I was. It said slowly: "Mike died in a car accident, two days ago. The autonomous vehicle he was riding in malfunctioned."

My heart seized; my scalp prickled; cold sweat slid down my back. "God! How could this happen?" The cry escaped before I could stop it.

The robot butler said only, mildly: "He rests in peace."

I stood in the doorway without moving.

I suddenly remembered what I had said to Mike the day I left the Dream Factory—"May you find peace." Now he had found peace, at the cost of his life. I didn't know whether his death had truly been an accident, or something arranged. All I could do now was pray that he had truly found peace.

The butler waited a moment, then asked softly: "Is there anything else I can help you with?"

"No," I said. "Thank you. Goodbye."

I don't know how I got home. I felt hollowed out, body and mind, and collapsed onto the bed the moment I walked in. Perhaps it was the accumulated sleepless nights, plus blow after blow landing on my nerves without pause—I drifted into a murky sleep. In my dream I dimly saw concrete pilings driven into the Lake of Heaven, steel mechanical arms on the pilings plunging into the water again and again, scooping up silt and dumping it onto transport barges. The machines roared; the birds overhead shrieked in terror. The lake's water was fouled to mud, dead fish floating belly-up on the surface, glinting a rancid yellow in a sunset red as blood. I jolted awake, my heart pounding so hard I could barely breathe. The dream was horrific—as if doomsday had come early. That pool of clarity that led to the ultimate reality, destroyed at last by human greed and selfishness; humanity bricking up, with its own hands, the door to the ultimate truth.

A sharp pain stabbed through my chest, and tears came. At some point Ace had appeared at my side with a glass of water. I pressed a hand to my chest and drank; my whole mouth tasted of bitterness. "A package arrived," Ace said quietly, holding out a courier envelope.

No sender. Only the delivery address—not even my name.

Baffled, I opened it. Inside was a smaller envelope. Inside that, a folded sheet of paper and a tiny chip.

I unfolded the paper. Two words: "The truth." Signed: "Woods."

I set the paper down, picked up the chip, set it down again.

Outside the window the sky had gone dark. I don't know how long I sat there. Ace said nothing, and didn't turn on the light.

At last I picked up the chip and slotted it into the computer.
